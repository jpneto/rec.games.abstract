hexagonal tiling of the projective plane

From: Nick Wedd <nick@maproom.co.uk>
Date: Wed, 17 Dec 2008 20:25:25 +0000

If you want to use a hexagonal grid on a projective plane for a game,
you will find you can't. There is no way to cover a projective plane
entirely with hexagons. Look up "Euler characteristic" if you want to
know why.

However, you can come fairly close to a hexagonal grid, you just need to
include a few smaller polygons. The most regular ways use two
triangles, or three squares, or six pentagons, and lots of hexagons.

Here is a way to build an arbitrarily large almost-hexagonal grid, with
just five pentagons among the hexagons. Start with a truncated
icosahedron: it has 12 pentagonal faces, 20 hexagonal, and 60 vertices.
Replace each pentagon by a smaller pentagon, centred on the original and
rotated through 36 degrees relative to it; replace each hexagon by a
smaller hexagon, centred on the original and rotated through 30 degrees
relative to it; and replace each vertex by a hexagon. Repeat the
instructions of the previous sentence until you have enough hexagons.
Right, now for the difficult bit: we need to identify all the antipodal
pairs of faces, antipodal pairs of pentagons, and antipodal pairs of
vertices, ending up with a grid that has half as many of each. A way to
do this is to mark a set of edges that comes as close as possible to the
"equator" of our near-spherical polyhedron, cut along it, and discard
one half. Now mark each pair of opposite hex-edges of the resulting
near-hemisphere, to indicate that the hexes they belong to in fact
border each other. We have a projective plane, covered by a grid of
many hexagons and six pentagons. If we intend using it for a game, it
will be sensible to project it onto a plane and print it on paper.

If instead of six equally-spaced pentagons we would rather have three
evenly spaced squares, apply the same method, but start with a truncated
octahedron instead of a truncated icosahedron. Or for two triangles,
start with a truncated tetrahedron.
