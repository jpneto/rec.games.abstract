Siesta review and rules clarifications

From: ideefixe <ideefixe@earthlink.net>
Date: 04 06 2000 00:00:00 GMT

Siesta is an excellent game by Guido Hoffman (son of game designer Rudi
Hoffman) that in my opinion ought to be reaching a bigger audience. Not
only do the bits look good -- as no reviewer seems able to resist mentioning
-- but the particular geometric swirl produced as the game plays out is a
bit aesthetically fascinating in its own way.

Siesta falls into the category of games with no hidden information and no
dice, but it is quite different from any other game! Durch die Wuste is a
starting point for comparisons, at least. You make a choice of available
markers to put on the board -- three of three types in Siesta, two of five
colors in Durch die Wuste -- and the game ends when you use the last of a
type or color. Thinking up what to do on your turn takes about the same
amount of time in either game, but in Siesta there'll be more turns: it's a
bigger board. For me personally, I was a lot more entertained watching the
Siesta game unfold than I am with Durch die Wuste; of course that's just a
matter of taste.

Unfortunately, the English translation of the Siesta rules has some problems
so bad as to make it almost impossible to figure out how the game is
supposed to be played. I know a couple of shots have been taken at making
sense of the game here on rec.board.games, and that has been appreciated!
In fact, without your help, we'd likely never have reached the point of
thinking we'd got the rules in shape to be played properly.

The worst problems with the rules arise from their description of a double
siesta: "If a player scores shadows in two Siestas on his turn, he receives
a bonus of 2 points," etc. This section is completely misleading, a
complete mess, and has to be thrown out and trampled in the dust (use
hobnail boots if you've got them) if the rules are to make any sense.

Other problems arose from wondering whether a siesta could have blank spaces
between sun and roof and/or between roof and shadow -- we eventually decided
not -- or whether in the situation:

(sun)-(roof)-(shadow)-(roof)-(shadow)

the second roof and/or shadow were part of a siesta. Again, we eventually
decided not.

To summarize, our analysis of the confounding parts of the rules -- and the
way we believe the game's designer must actually intend it to be played --
goes like this:

A siesta is any straight line of pieces on the board that goes
sun-roof-shadow, with no empty spaces in between -- but each of the three
elements (sun, roof, and shadow) may appear in multiples. Thus you can have
sun-sun-roof-roof-shadow or sun-roof-shadow-shadow-shadow or whatever.

We can depict this as (sun x n)-(roof x n)-(shadow x n).

The roofs in a siesta do not have to be the same color (that is, do not have
to belong to the same player). Each siesta scores the number of shadows it
contains for each of the players who has a roof in it. Note, however, that
each player scores that number of shadows only once, no matter how many
roofs he has in the siesta.

A shadow may wind up belonging to as many as four siestas, since the board
is two-dimensional and can be read equally left-to-right, right-to-left,
top-to-bottom, and bottom-to-top.

A double siesta consists of a straight line (sun x n)-(roof x n)-(shadow x
n)-(roof x n)-(sun x n) -- that is, a series of pieces in which all the
shadows belong to two siestas from opposite directions -- with the special
additional requirement that all the roofs in both siestas must belong to the
same player. This pays a 2-point bonus in addition to the points scored for
each siesta.

For example, sun-redroof-shadow-shadow-shadow-redroof-redroof-sun-sun is a
double siesta and scores a total of 8 points: 3 for the siesta starting on
the left, 3 for the siesta starting on the right, plus the 2-point bonus for
double siesta.

In contrast, sun-redroof-shadow-shadow-shadow-greenroof-redroof-sun-sun is
not a double siesta because all the roofs are not the same color. Red still
scores 3 for the siesta starting on the left plus 3 for the siesta starting
on the right, but does not get the 2-point bonus since there is no double
siesta. Green also scores 3 for the siesta starting on the right.

Despite what the rules translation says, it is perfectly acceptable for a
double siesta to include a shadow which scores for another player on the
perpendicular, even in the case where the opposing player scores on the same
play when the double siesta is formed. Also despite what the rules
translation says, merely scoring in two different siestas during your turn
does not constitute a double siesta.

We do follow the instruction that "Shadows must always be played so they are
part of one or more Siestas," that is, that each shadow played must score
for some player -- but not necessarily for the player making the play!

You do, of course, have to score at least one point for yourself with the
three pieces played on your turn.

It is a big help to bear in mind that all the scoring stays on the board, so
that at the end of the game, you can total it all up again if you need to to
make sure you got it right. With a little practice and attention to detail,
though, you'll be able to keep the running totals accurate. This is a good
thing especially when playing with more than two players, as you may have to
choose between plays that help one opponent or another more.

Here's hoping this'll help get a few more players interested in this fine
game!
