> My intuitive guess would be that uniform random events that

Is that a standard notion of complexity? It sounds like you are saying that
the complexity is probability of winning with optimal play divided by the
probability of winning with random play. Complexity would then decrease with
a greater number of trivial decisions, with more obviously bad choices. I'd
like to try to cast a bit more light on the problem.

Suppose I play chess against a perfect opponent. Each position can be
labelled 0, 1/2, or 1, depending on whether with perfect play I will lose,
draw, or win. I will make at most two errors in the whole game, e.g., by
achieving a position with value 1/2 when I could have still had a winning
position.

Suppose I play backgammon (without the doubling cube) against a perfect
opponent. Each position can be labelled by a number between 0 and 1, the
expected chance of winning if I play perfectly. Now I can make many
mistakes. In particular, after making several mistakes, I may still have a
chance to win which can be thrown away by making more mistakes.

Amusingly, I played a game of chess against someone on a game server that
has both games. I won with black, and refused a rematch in order to play
backgammon. I invited my opponent, who said that chess is a harder game, and
a game of pure skill. If there is no luck in chess, why did he want a
rematch?

Some moves in chess are considered stronger than others. Against a perfect
opponent, there can be at most three levels of moves: correct ones, ones
which lose 1/2 point, and ones that lose a full point. So to say that it is
bad to throw away one's queen while in a lost position, or to rank several
moves by strength, suggests subjective randomness. These issues make up most
of the game of chess to me. How does that fit into your model?

Another example: Tic-tac-toe is equivalent to a game in which two players in
turn select numbers from 1 to 9, trying to get 3 numbers which add up to 15.
Abstractly, these should have the same complexity, but people find the
latter harder. Are you sure chess is abstractly complex? Perhaps one should
try to bound the complexity of a formula which tells one a correct move, but
there is an abstractly equivalent game which has, as a winning formula,
"Choose the leftmost node."
