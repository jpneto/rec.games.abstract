> To conclude that there are differences in skill level, you need to show a

Chess is also different in that draws are possible. Indeed, between
players of exactly equal skill, even with the advantage to White, the
majority of games should end in draws.

But in general the original poster's (unstated) theory is correct: Even
if a game does involve skill, the outcomes from a small set of matches
between equally skilled players will tend toward a bell curve. If you
want to draw conclusions about relative skills, you need to recognise
that the disparity in results may be due to random effects (which can
arise from player decisions and errors even if the game itself has no
random element).

The same sort of issue can arise in analysing choices in a game,
especially one with random elements. E.g., at some point in a game
I'm faced with two promising moves. I write a computer simulation
that plays out the game two million times: in half of them I make one
move; in the other half I make the alternative move. The million
simulations for a given move don't all give the same results, due to
random elements or due to choices made later in each simulated game.
Given the percentage of wins after each of the two possible moves,
I want to decide whether one move is significantly better than the
other. To decide that, I have to figure out if the difference I got
is likely due to chance.

Fortunately, if you do enough simulations, the accuracy of the outcome
does get better. But it's a square-root effect: if you do 100 times
as many simulations, your accuracy improves by a factor of 10. I.e.,
if the outcome of N simulations is 95% likely to be within a range of
size R, then the outcome of N*100 simulations is 95% likely to be
within a range of size R/10. (To a first approximation, at least.)

In the original poster's experiment, if he repeated the experiment he'd
probably get a similar bell curve, but each individual "player" would
end up in a very different (i.e. random) place on the curve compared to
the first time. Whereas if the simulated players were NOT of equal
skill, there should be some tendency (a) to have the curve be more
spread out and (b) to have individual players end up in about the same
place if the experiment is repeated. And these effects should be more
pronounced if each player plays 100 or 10000 games vs each other player.
