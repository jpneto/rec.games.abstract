>1) You may not use the same number your opponent just used, or 9 minus

Nor -7 or -2.

>2) Every multiple of 10 is a "hoop" which must be passed through ending
>at the same distant from that hoop as the turn began. For example, if
>your score were 17 and you wanted to go through the 2 hoop, you would
>have to choose 6 to end at 23 (23-20=20-17). Yyou may go through the
>hoop in two moves, but you must still obey the same-distance
>restriction. 38, then 40, then 42, for example. If you are forced to go

including the 100 hoop

And, you can only do this once---the second time you pass a hoop
invalidly, you lose immediately.

> To go backwards pick a number following the first
>restriction, but subtract it from your score. You do not have to follow
>the same-distance restriction when going backward. You only go backwards
>until you've gotten below the hoop you faulted on.

You must get below the hoop in one step.

>
>The 100 hoop must be passed through, not simply landed on. And that's

If you were on a hoop, and couldn't play the number you needed to get
off, you could pass.

Also, if the second player and first player win on the same turn, the
game is a draw (i.e., second player is guaranteed as many turns as the
first).

Comments:

Sorry to disappoint you, but the game *is* trivial---at least by
computer; the search space is too small.

The contest was only interesting because of strict resource limitations
(very little prepared data allowed, little dynamic RAM, and little
CPU). Nevertheless, several players had programs capable of playing
perfectly entered.

Why did I win? Mostly luck. But also, the REAL challenging part of
the contest turns out to be ambiguous rules and, even harder, no access
to the target platform. I had to write code for a computer I don't
have, to be compiled by a compiler I've never seen, to interface to
code I don't have.

I and another player got it right (through luck more than skill, I
think); several other "perfect" programs got bit by computer or
compiler differences, or by failing to correctly guess the exact
definition of the rules or interface.

I beat the other perfect program because an imperfect program managed
to play perfectly against my competitor but make an error against me.

Actually, I submitted two entries; only one of them worked, the other
suffered from one of those annoying platform differences.

The game is a draw with perfect play.

And, yes, it can be played by humans without pencil or paper---I
sincerely doubt any human can play the game perfectly, and the state is
very small. Unfortunately, it's a very *dull* game, in my personal
opinion. Playing "Geography" is more fun. :-).

----------------------

Aside: Programming contests are fun, but they should not be exercises
in parsing ambiguous documents and guessing at compiler quirks.

My STRONG suggestion for anyone planing on running such a contest in
the future is that you should develop the referee code and test it on
several platforms, and make it public well before the contest (at least
a month). The published referee program can then be the definitive
standard of what the rules of the game are, and the interface to be
used.

Secondly, entrants should be allowed to submit entries in advance of
the contest date for testing on the target hardware.

If such had been done in the Croquar contest, I probably would not have
won... which is how it should have been; there were better designed
and written programs than my own.
