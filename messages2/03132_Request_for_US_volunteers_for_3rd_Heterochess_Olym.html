Request for US volunteers for 3rd Heterochess Olympics

From: ucalegon@aol.com (Ucalegon)
Date: 25 11 1995 00:00:00 GMT

In the last two Olympics, all the US players (and the one Canadian) were
members of the postal game club NOST, but this doesn't have to be the
case.
I'd be happy to see non-NOST join us. But if you want to sign up, keep in
mind:

E-mail is much less common on the other side of the pond, particularly in
Eastern European countries. You will have to play most of your games by
snailmail; the minimum cost is 50 cents for an airmail postcard or
aerogramme.

You probably won't get to play your favorite game. There will be a number
of
games to play, most of which will not be the favorite of any US players,
but
someone has to play them. We need generalists. We particularly need
players of
games played in Scottish/Progressive chess style (White makes 1 move,
Black 2, White 3, etc.), since their speed makes them well-suited for
international
play, and several are likely to be proposed.
The games in the second Olympics, with rule sketches and proposers, were:
Avalanche (each move consists of two parts: first, a legal orthochess
move,
then pushing an opponent's pawn one square forward) (North America)
Balanced Marseillais (two moves per turn, except one on White's first)
(Italy 1)
English Progressive (like Progressive, above, but no piece can move a
second
time in a turn unless all mobile pieces have moved) (Ukraine)
Giveaway (object is to give away all 16 pieces; stalemate is a draw)
(Italy 2)
Progressive (see above) (Poland)
Progressive Circe (captured pieces are reborn on their start squares;
progressive cadence (England)
Progressive Mutation (a capturing piece changes to the rank of the
capturee;
progressive cadence) (Esperanto)
Triplets (play pawn, piece, and king moves, one each, every turn after the
first two) (Czech & Slovak republics)
Note that only Giveaway has only one piece moving per turn, and it's sped
up
by long forced sequences.

And, if you'll forgive my saying so, I probably don't know anything about
you,
so I'd like to know something about your bona fides. In the second
Olympics,
two of the seven NA players rudely vanished without a word, which was
embarrassing and troublesome for the rest of us. One decamped early enough
that others could take his place, but the other, who I thought I could
trust,
got us a lot of forfeits. If you've never played postally, now is not the
time
to start.

I hope that hasn't scared everyone off. Join us!

Philip M. Cohen (ucalegon@aol.com)
