Again, I'll suggest that there may be a world of difference between a
perfect game and a flawless one. (As you'll recall, this whole discussion
started when someone posted a message asking if we could create a perfect
game by the year 2000; but he left out his definition of
"perfect"--leaving us to choose between "flawless" and "ideal.")
Most people seem to think the *ideally* perfect game would be one
that everyone in the world would enjoy immensely--a game whose popularity
would make all other games obsolete.
A *flawlessly* perfect game, in contrast, would be a finely balanced
game with clean, clear, exception-free rules.
To my way of thinking, designing the latter type of game would be a
pointless exercise. Even if it were feasible, the result would likely be
a sterile mathematical game that no one would ever bother playing.
Something like tic-tac-toe with simultaneous movement.
But I also doubt the validity of equating "ideally perfect" with
"overwhelmingly popular." That's based on the premise that the purpose of
games is simply to amuse people. And I wonder about that. I think
there's more to it.
Do serious chess players always find the game entertaining, amusing,
or fun? And if "Dungeons & Dragons" has brought more emotional
satisfaction to people than chess ever did in its long history, does that
mean D&D is necessarily a superior game? I don't think so.
So, if we're looking for the "ideally perfect" game, I think we
should first consider the *best* games in the world. The choice of which
games are best is a subjective question, of course, and could be endlessly
debated. But if history and popularity are any indication, I'd say three
games we should consider are chess, go, and bridge. Those three games
evidently have a certain something that most other games lack. Something
that has kept them immensely popular for a long span of time, throughout
large parts of the world.
One common feature of those three games is that they're all simple
enough to quickly learn and play, yet complex enough that (1) they provide
a lifetime of challenge to the above-average intellect and (2) they seem
to mean something.
Being simple and playable is a prerequisite for any good game. But
being complex *in the right way* is, I think, what makes for a *great*
game. Just being challenging is not enough; puzzles and math problems are
challenging but not always interesting. In fact, too much challenge is
daunting and can spoil a game.
So the key question is, What makes a game interesting or meaningful?
I'm not sure I can put my finger on the answer exactly--but I'd say it has
something to do with the game somehow seeming to be a metaphor for life,
or some facet of life. Chess seems like a stylized battle; go seems like
a whole war or territorial competition; bridge seems like big business.
And battle, war, or business, in turn, provide another level of
metaphor--so that, in the end, the principles of the game are indirectly
applicable to all of life.
An excellent game, therefore, is *indirectly* a metaphor for all
life. And by extension, a *perfect* game would be the ideal (albeit
indirect, simplified, and polished) symbolic representation of life.
A merely flawless game, in contrast, would likely be too abstract to
provide any symbolism at all. It would be sterile and negative in
meaning. It would be a metaphor for death, not life.
