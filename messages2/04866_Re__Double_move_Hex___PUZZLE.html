I played some games from micro to larger boards, and here are two reasons:

(1) The "doublet" move, if not disallowed, breaks the basic "bridge"
structure of Hex (which I call the "stride" in my games) while I was
trying to preserve the flavor of Hex. Interestingly, mobility/extension
becomes greater (the "jump" is connected) and simple extension by
doublets is too hard to stop -- leading to a futile doublet-ridden
defense of doublet attack. DOUBLET ALLOWED:

x The stride . . But the
. . is not a x . x jump is a
x connection. . . connection!

(2) Disallowing the doublet introduces ko (or enhances? I have always
thought Hex ko-ish but can't explain it). The game is still Hex but
"more so". DOUBLET DISALLOWED:

o a . . White (o) to defend but . x o White (o) to attack and
x b a cannot play doublet so a b o plays a; black defends
x o . plays a; black plays b x . o with b and tries to
and somewhere else. a x . attack somewhere else.

I considered disallowing even the bridge (stride) in a double-move but
tight positions became nearly unplayable, and defense suffered. Possibly,
one might disallow an open bridge ("2-connected") while allowing a
(partially) blocked one.

Hex is a paradoxical game -- offense being too strong leads to defensive
strategies...

Aside: double-move variants typically are slugfests; witness Abalone which
is a stalemate under standard rules but FUN under double-move.

I recommend playing doublet-free, double-move Hex on a larger board than
usual.
