Tactics generally require these strategical advantages in order
to be succesful, and thus, these elements begin to have worth.
Tactics can be the means by which a strategic advantage is forged,
since strategic disadvantage is a concession in itself.

Perhaps it's easiest to think of it this way:

Tactics involves forward (ie chronological) thinking.
I go here, he goes there, bing, bang, bong, take, chop, chop,
and swoosh, it's mate (this was overheard by a master who
was involved in a multi-player post-mortem after having to
settle for a draw versus an IM). This, you will agree, is
a crucial part of chess.

Strategy involves backward (or reversed) thinking.
This is where a lucrative position is forseen without
any knowledge of how to get there. Sometimes strategy
can be one move deep (ie we see that a bishop is better
posted on b5, and we immediately play Bb5). Sometimes,
this can be quite involved -- particularly in endings
where it is so crucial to fully consolidate your position
before racing to realize a minute advantage.

Somebody once said that a very deep tactic is not
destinguishable to strategy. It's true that an
observer may not know by what means such a move was
found, but to the player, there is a big difference
in how one goes about finding such moves.
