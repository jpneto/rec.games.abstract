> A quick history lesson on the game of Hex:

> The proof goes like this: Either there is a winning strategy for the first
> player, or there is a winning strategy for the second player.

...because draws are impossible!

> Suppose there were a winning strategy for the second player. The first player
> could then "steal" this strategy by making an arbitrary first move and then
> pretending to be the second player from then on. Whenever the strategy would
> call for playing on a hexagon he already occupied (e.g. because that was his
> arbitrary first play), the first player would just make another arbitrary
> move. His extra stone could never hurt him. Thus, the first player would
> win, which is a contradiction, therefore the second player does not have a
> winning strategy, therefore the first player does.
>
>
>
> Now, just as in the game Hex, having an extra peg on the board in Twixt can
> never hurt you. Thus, like Hex, there probably does indeed exist a winning
> strategy for the first player in Twixt, but this has not yet been found.
> (Note however that unlike Hex, draws ARE possible in Twixt. Thus, it's
> possible the first player may not have a winning strategy... with correct play
> Player 2 may be able to draw.)
>
> But yes, the first player does have an advantage, which is why Twixt, like
> Hex, should always be played with the swap rule.

Which of course increases the probability that in TwixT,
perfect play indeed gives a draw! :-)
