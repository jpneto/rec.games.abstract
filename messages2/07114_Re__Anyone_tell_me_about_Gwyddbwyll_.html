Generally most sources I've read seem to associate Fidchell with
tafl-style games, Although some do connect it to Ludus latrunculorum, so
it is certainly subject to debate. To add to the confusion, according to
game historian David Parlett, by the 15th century the Gaelic word
Fidchell had commonly become adopted to refer to Chess. In the modern
Irish spelling, Ficheall can now be used as a generic for any kind of
wooden board game. In fact, in the Wooing of Etain, quoted below, the
translators actually used the word Chess rather than "Fidchell."

What is thy name? said Eochaid.
Not famous, said he, Midir of Bri Leith.
What has brought thee? said Eochaid.
To play Fidchell with thee, said he.
Of a truth I am good at Fidchell, said Eochaid.
Let us make trial of it, said Midir.
The queen is asleep, said Eochaid, and it is in her house that the
Fidchell board is.
I have here, said Midir, a Fidchell board that is not inferior. That
was true: a silver board and golden men, and each corner there lit up by
precious stones, and a bag for the men of plaited links of bronze.
-The Wooing of Etain Translated by Osborn Bergin, and Richard Irvine
Best, 1937.

Here's a little more on Gwyddbwyll from my reading

Gwyddbwyll, and Tawlbyrdd are both referred to in Welsh sagas.
Fortunately a manuscript from 1587 in the Welsh National Library solves
the mystery in regard to Tawlbwrdd. The description is unmistakably a
Tafl-style game set on an 11x11 grid: The king in the center of the
board and twelve men in the place next to him, and twenty-four lie in
wait to capture him -with the 24 attackers equally spaced on the four
edges of the board. The term Gwyddbwyll seems to be older than
Tawlbwrdd. Part of the theory in regard to Latrunculorum is that
Gwyddbwyll was its Welsh counterpart, and that Tawlbwrdd may have been a
Tafl game imported by Norse and/or Saxon invaders and gradually replaced
Gwyddbwyll.

Here's a few other British/Celtic game names that are a puzzle to modern
researchers.
"Brandubh" (Black Raven) appears in the Irish sagas as early as the 12th
century, one such source describes the use of 5 men against 8. This
would seem well-suited to a small Tafl-like game played on a small grid
like the Ballinderry board, or similar engraved boards found on the Orkneys.

"Buanfach," "Cennchaem," "Conchobair," "Ard-ri"

In the end, we have to remember that these were probably folk games
whose rules could vary from one village to the next. We only have to
look at the dozens of versions of Wari/Mancala to wonder if it is
possible that all the names above were simply regional terms for just
one or two games or their variants.
