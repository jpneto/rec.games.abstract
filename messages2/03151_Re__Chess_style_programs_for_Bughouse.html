In article <4atat2$fak@news.microsoft.com>, Bruce Moreland <brucemo> wrote:

>1) You have one clock per team, rather than one per board.
>
>2) When you make a move and hit your clock, it still runs until your
>partner also makes a move and hits the clock.
>
>3) Your opponent can't make a move until your clock has stopped, meaning
>that both members of a team must have moved before either opponent is allowed
>to move.
>
>I would call this "synchronous bughouse". This removes all race conditions.

No, it doesn't: Now a player has to wonder about what his partner is
going to do, and when.

Works as a two-player version, but with four players there's race
conditions, so it's not an abstract game---but it's closer than
traditional bughouse is, for sure.

But since it's about the same as my first proposal in terms of
synchronization, and adds race conditions, I'm not sure it's an
improvement: Why not just add "both players on a team share one clock"
to my proposal?

I've been thinking about quantifying the time aspect of traditional
bughouse: start each player with a number of tokens, and a turn
consists of everybody who is on move specifying a move to make, and a
bid number of tokens (minimum zero). This is written down and kept
secret until everybody is ready.

Person(s) who bid lowest removes that number of tokens, and moves.
Everybody else removes the (low bid + 1) tokens.

It's not abstract (you don't know what your opponent or partner will
bid) but it's well quantified, and should be more easily attacked and
analyzed than traditional bughouse.

This version at least retains some of the traditional "fun" parts of
bughouse: the game on one board moving faster than on the other, with
time (stalling factor) being finite.

I haven't come up with a way to make the game purely abstract *and*
keep the possibility of the two games proceeding at disjoint rates.
