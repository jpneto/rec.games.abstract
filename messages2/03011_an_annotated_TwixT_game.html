TwixT e-mail game: Ken Balakrishnan vs. David Bush, Spring 1995
(posted with Ken's permission)

Row 1 and row 24 are Red's border rows.
Column A and column X are Black's border columns.

Red Black

1. M12 A solid opening move. Playing midway between his walls, Red
makes Black's choice of which way to go around as difficult as
possible.
M7 Perhaps not the best, since it is below the "crucial diagonal"
leading to the B2 corner: B2-D3-F4-H5-J6-L7-N8...
2. Q8 The opening is rarely a time for linking. Instead, Red is
spreading his pegs around the board, making as many potential
paths for his bridge as possible. He is also blocking Black,
just as Black blocked Red with M7.
L16 Following opening principles, Black also spreads out, giving Red
maximum opportunity to leave a weak spot in his "net."
3. H16 By playing on the left, Red is NOT absolutely committing himself
to building a bridge there. Instead, the THREAT of a bridge on
the left forces Black to respond. To borrow a term from Go, Red
has the "sente," which means initiative.
I14 At first, I (as Black) thought that this move effectively
neutralized Red's H16 peg. As will be seen later, this is not
the case. But I14 was probably my best shot. Red is winning.
4. O16 P13 Black threatens to cut through either between M12 and Q8, or
between M12 and O16.
5. N14 I was planning to answer 5. O9 or something similar with N17.
(Note that any link that can be made is automatically made,
unless stated otherwise.)
N9 I believe this is better than O11, because it makes the winning
path for Red less obvious. Conway calls this the "enough rope"
principle.
6. O11 Red could have played K8! now or on move 7- see below.
P8 This specific tactical shot, I call "hammering" Red's Q8 peg.
If we were closer to Black's wall, Black's threat of linking
past Q8 on either one side or the other would be devastating,
as far as that local battle is concerned.

Up to here, Red's play has been just fine. Now is the time to play the
entire board instead of getting drawn into the local battle around Q8. Red
can win here with the not-so-obvious 7. K8! Other moves in the same region
might also work, but K8 is the one I mainly looked at. At first glance, it
seems to be a lousy move, but it turns out that nothing works against it.
The basic reason for this is that Red can use the threat of connecting to
his M12 group and at the same time sneak around the left, hooking up with
his H16 peg to reach the bottom. And the top wall is just too close for
Black to build a net. Here are some possible continuations:

(1) 7. K8! L10 8. J10 K12 9. I12 I13 10. H14 wins.
(2) 7. ... J8 8. I9 J6 9. H7 H5 10. D6 gets it.
(3) 7. ... L8 8. I9 is similar to line (2).
(4) 7. ... J6 8. J5 L7 9. I7 K9 10. J10 is similar to (1).
(5) 7. ... M18 8. R19 N5 9. I9! Black is toasted dogmeat.
(6) 7. ... I4 8. K4 L5 9. I5 H6 10. H7! wins.

Returning to the game:

7. S9? If the battle to keep Black from connecting to the right above
Red's M12 group were the only battle on the board, then S9 would
win. In fact, this is probably the losing move.
Q20! To borrow a term from chess, this move is a zwischenzug, which
is German for "intermediate move." It makes a threat which must
be answered, and simultaneously improves Black's position
elsewhere. In this case, I'm providing enough support to punch
through between Red's Q8 and O11 pegs.
8. N18 Red might conceivably have had a shot with something like
8. E13, but 8. ... H12 9. F11 G7 seems to stop it.
N5! Another zwischenzug. This time, I'm threatening to cut across
the top by playing S5, and at the same time taking the sting out
(although just barely) of the threat of K8 or something similar.
9. P6 Q10
10. R12 S11 was also a valiant defense. Losing, but valiant.
Q11

At this point, 11. K8 is still a good move. Black is only just able to
scrape by. Here is what looks like the main line: 11. K8 H4 12. J5!
(alternatives: 12. K4 L4 13. I5 I6 14. L2 J8; 12. J6 J3 13. L4 M4 14. M6 K3;
12. F5 H10 Black threatens to break through 2 different ways. A move such as
this will be Black's saving grace in many variations. Or 12. H5 J8 13. I7 L9
14. J9 K11 15. I11 J12 16. K13 L11) 12. ... K6 13. I7 J4 14. F5 E5 15. D6 G4
16. C4 (If 16. E4 then Black responds E3 to avoid a draw) 16. ... H11! (The
more natural-looking H10 fails against 17. L10 F6 18. E8 G8 19. E13!) Now Red
has several ways to lose:
(1)17. L10 F6 18. E8 G8 19. E13 F12;
(2)17. H6 L10 18. J10 K12;
(3)17. D11 E14 could probably be inserted in many of these lines;
(4)17. I10 I9 18. H12 G13 19. H6 L10 20. K9 (20. K11 K10 21. L9 L8)
20. ... J11 21. K13 I13 and Black will connect to the G13 group or will
punch through to the left wall directly;
(5)17. H9 L10 18. I11 K13 19. K12 L11 20. R11! Red gets to make a zwischenzug.
This one threatens to win on the right, and simultaneously tries to force a
draw in the middle of the board. 20. ... S12 21. P9 R9 22. M9 L12! (with an
unstoppable connection to L10) 23. L14 K14 (the L16 peg actually gets used
in this variation!) 24. I13 G15 25. J15 K15 with a link either via J17 or
J13. If Red played O10 instead of P9, the K13-L11 link should remain until
L12 connects with L10.
(6)17. I9 F6 18. E8 G8 19. G9 H10 (screee!) 20. F12 G15 21. G14 E14 22. I13
J12 23. G10 (23. J11 F11) 23. ... J9 24. J10 (24. I11 K11) 24. ... I11
25. K12 H13 (unlink H11-J12, link I11-H13-J12) 26. I3 I2 27. F3 G2 28. D4
H5 29. G3 (stopping one double-link but not the other) F4 (removing
E5-G4-F6, linking H5-F4-G2) and now I might as well throw in Red's best
defense on the right side: 30. T13 S15 31. Q14 Q15 32. T17 R17 33. U15 S21,
Black is battered but unbowed.
