: I have recently started working thru Dvoretsky's "Secrets of Chess Tactics".
: Here he poses real problems embedded in actual games. He asks you
: to evaluate critical positions where combinations might arise.
: Sometimes they work and sometimes they don't. I confess this book
: is somewhat too hard for me - it's probably aimed at Master level
: players, but working thru it and doing the exercises as best as I
: can seems to have made a big difference to my play.

This is a valuable book, but I'd have to say: read it for the advice and
forget about the exercises. As a 2300 player I only managed to get 3 or 4 of
the exercises in the whole book, and I looked at some of them for about a
half an hour! A lot of the problem is that my approach to calculation is not
well organized, and that visualization gives me trouble; often when I've
looked at enough different lines, pieces magically reappear on the board
(they rarely disappear) and result in wrong evaluations, including those in
which I simply misevaluated the material balance!

I don't feel so bad about my troubles with these exercises, though, because I
met Dvoretsky and he admitted that the exercises were extremely hard. (He
also showed me a position in which he opined Kasparov wouldn't have found the
optimal solution in an OTB game!) However, he also said that it is useful to
have examples for training purposes, and he said that most of the available
books are full of errors. Sometimes these are analytical errors, and
sometimes they give you a position that is so good that you may prefer
alternative methods of winning and don't need to see the 9-ply combination
that wins a pawn!
