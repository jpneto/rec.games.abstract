Forget about standards that don't fit into reality. Gala has two "kings",
Chu Shogi has a potential second "king" and in Tamerlane's Chess there
is a potential second and even a potential third "king" (called
"prince" and "adventititious king" in the English translation).
But you are right: USUALLY (but not NECESSARILY) a chess variant has only
one "king". But this only defines a further subcategory within the family
of chess variants: you have variants with only one king (i would call this
family "mono-chess") and you have variants with at least the potential of
several "kings" ("poly-chess"!!!). Gess is a modern poly-chess variant.



> i.e. a piece that is not taken, but that the game
> ends when it cannot avoid capture on the next move.

This is modern western chess. In eastern chess variants as well as in
Shatranj you are (were) allowed to capture (kill) the opponent's "king".
You can move the king into check or capture with your king and -- at
the same time -- move into check.
Usually, however, this is not considered a good idea, because you would
lose the game. However, this can be a good move in variants which has
at least two two "kings".

> Games can also have multiple goals.

Only some few. Havannah is an example (connection game AND a forming
geometric shape). If have described 120 games in my abstract games book
project, and I think there are only three hybrids. Don't forget that there
are final goals (which decide who has won the game; Go --> territory) and
instrumental goals (goals that help you to achieve the final goal; Go -->
connection). My classification system is only based on the final goal.

One thing we could discuss further: Draughts has a primary final goal -->
capture all opponent's men, but also a secondary final goal --> blockade
your opponent so that he cannot move. I decided to ommit this secondary
goal in my classification of board games. However, this secondary goal I
just mentioned is the primary and only final goal in some abstract games,
e.g. Juego de los Amazonas or Forge. I describe these games as a
particular sub-group of territory games (!). I think that the ability to
make moves is a subtle "measure" of territory.
