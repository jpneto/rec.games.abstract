montereygo@aol.com (MONTEREYGO) writes:
> What this thread has gotten me to wondering is: how can you determine if a
> game can't be solved?

By stealing somebody else's theory :-) John von Neumann proved (70
years ago!) that every finite, two-person, zero-sum game has a value
(which is equivalent to the definition of a "solution" posted here
earlier; it also implies there exists at least one optimal strategy
for each player, and a few other interesting properties). Therefore,
for a game to have no solution, it is necessary (but not sufficient)
for it to either involve more than two players; not be strictly
competitive (zero-sum); or have an infinite number of pure strategies.

Examples of games that have no solutions:

- Both players simultaneously announce an integer of their choice; the
player choosing the larger integer wins. (This violates the condition
that a finite number of pure strategies must exist. Interestingly, the
cases where the strategies can be mapped onto a closed interval of real
numbers, and the "value functions" of the games are continuous over these
strategies, are examples of games which _do_ have solutions even though
there are infinite strategies available.)

- "The Prisoner's Dilemna" (this has been discussed extensively elsewhere;
essentially two suspects have been accused of the same crime; they must
each decide whether to confess or plead innocence -- the catch is that
they must decide independently, with no knowledge of the other prisoner's
decision. The sentences they will receive are such that there is some
incentive to encourage each prisoner to betray the other by rewarding
a prisoner if he is the only one of the pair that confesses.) This
violates the condition that the game be zero-sum; it's not strictly
competitive. "The Battle of the Sexes" is another canonical example.

- Games for more than two players. These are considerably more complex
because of the possibility of collusion between players; one consequence
is that games cannot be assumed to be strictly competitive. This has
some very interesting consequences: for instance the nature of the game
may become quite different if communication is permitted before the game
starts. In fact it may be detrimental to you to even allow another player
to communicate a message to you! There has actually been a significant
amount of work in this area, but there appear to be no simple conclusions
in the same way that there were for finite, two-person, zero-sum games.
