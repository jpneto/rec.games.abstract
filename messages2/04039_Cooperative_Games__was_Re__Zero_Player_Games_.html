I for one don't think the idea of a cooperative game sounds
illogical at all. In most cases, a completely cooperative game
would probably be closest to "multi-player solitaire" (now there's
an oxymoron), but that doesn't make it any less of a game.

(Speaking of the definition of game vs. the definition of
puzzles or toys, read "I Have No Words & I Must Design" by
Greg Costikyan if you haven't already. It's at
http://www.crossover.com/~costik/nowords.html.
It's kind of RPG-oriented, but applicable to this discussion.)

If you can find it, check out "Beyond Competition", a book by
Sid Sackson that is subtitled "Six Dynamic New Games for Two
or More Players to Win Together". There is no inter-player
competition in any of the games -- All players either win
together or lose together. Essentially, the mechanics of
the game are the only opponent. I don't remember any of the
games were being particularly astounding, but they were interesting.
For example, I believe one of the games was one where the players
had to guide a rocket ship to each of the 9 planets in order by
taking turns applying discrete movements to the rocket according
to some set of constraints. If they could do it within some
certain number of turns, they won. This example is admittedly
"puzzle-like", but given that no two playings were exactly the
same, I believe it did indeed qualify as a game.

As another poster pointed out, most multi-player (non-zero-sum)
games have strong cooperative elements embedded into the
competition, but it should be noted that some games have
cooperative objectives explicitly defined alongside the
competitive victory conditions. An example of a partially-
cooperative game of this sort that particularly appeals to
me is Avalon Hill's "Republic of Rome". For those not familiar
with it, the players control different factions of senators in
republican Rome. They compete to acquire the most influence,
popularity, military strength, and wealth, and there are
several ways to win the game, but all throughout, the game
mechanics throw obstacles in Rome's path that players (as
the Senate) must cooperate against, at least to some degree.
There are several ways Rome can fall, and if it does, all
players lose.

Just some thoughts on the subject,
