Fascinating stuff. I have invented my own little mathematical game, which I
call 3-6-9 from the key numbers that it uses, in which there is a decided
advantage in taking the *last* move (it is bound to score at least 6
points). How does that work out as regards these infinite series?

<3-6-9 gen>
Play consists of filling in the squares of a 9x9 checkerboard. The centre
square is already filled in at the start of the game, so that each player
gets 40 turns each.
Filling in the 3rd square of any row, column or diagonal scores 1 point.
Filling in the 6th square of any row, column or diagonal scores 2 points
Filling in the 9th square of any row, column or diagonal scores 3 points.

If anybody is interested, let me know and I can pop a copy of a Windows
version of the game for play against the PC, where the squares are "filled
in" with randomly-chosen icons.
</3-6-9 gen>

> [snip the background]
> There is a very slight application to all this!
>
> It is a triviality that the usual method of playing games where there
> is an obvious first-move advantage, is unfair to the second player.
"First"
> is clearly an average of half a move ahead of "Second", e.g. at random
times.
> A typical attempted rectification of this, in abstract-game-playing
circles,
> is the 1 2 2 2 2 ... move transformer, whereby after the first move, each
> player plays two moves consecutively. Though inappropriate for most games
> if used directly, it may have its uses if some further restrictions are
added.
>
> It has the Cesaro-propery of "evening-out" the starting advantage, (though
> for VERY short games a further integration to 1 3 4 4 4 4 may be
suitable),
> and it is nice to see the sum of 1-2+2-... coming to 0 by almost every
method.
>
>
> Now, another move transformer often used is the "Progressive" transformer,
> whereby the moves are taken in series of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 etc. It makes for
> fun games, if hardly very serious ones; and e.g. Progressive Chess already
> has quite a long history.
>
> But it often struck me that even so, there was a very slight advantage to
First.
> (e.g. His number of moves ahead is successively 1 -1 2 -2 3 -3... so that
First
> is always first to get to a new number of moves ahead, rather than
Second.)
> And so it now appears this advantage is real! There is an advantage to
1/4
> of a move to First!!! Horrors! This must not be!!
>
> So one way I have considered for some while of rectifying the Progressive
> transformer was to make it an "Odd-Progressive" transformer. This has
move
> series of 1, 3, 5, 7... , which gives the number of moves First is ahead
> each time as being 1 -2 3 -4... , which is clearly fairer than the above.
>
> And what do we see? The above methods give that
>
> 1 - 3 + 5 - 7 + 9 - = 0 EXACTLY!
>
> What a triumph!
>
> The Odd Progressive transformer is the answer to young Sagme Trabsact's
prayer!!
>
