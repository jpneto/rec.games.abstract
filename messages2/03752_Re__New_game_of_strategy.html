astephan@students.uiuc.edu (adam louis stephanides) writes:

>I think there is a bit of confusion here. The argument for a first-
>player winning strategy for Hex has nothing to do with any spatial
>reflection. It is a reductio ad absurdum: assume the second player
>has a winning strategy; then the first player can win by playing
>the first move arbitrarily, then following the winning strategy for
>the second player as if that move were not on the board (if the
>strategy calls on the first player to move on the hex occupied
>on the first move, play another arbitrary move, etc.).

>But this doesn't work for Wiggen's game on a fixed board, since
>in that game, unlike in Hex, having an extra piece on the board
>can hurt you (it might end up as a disconnected region by itself).
>And even if the argument did work, it would only show that the
>first player could at least draw, since draws are possible in
>Wiggen's game but not in Hex.

It would appear I spoke too soon. On thinking it over, it seems
that my first objection is only valid when the board has an odd
number of spaces. When the number of spaces is even, this argument
does indeed show that the first player has at least a drawing
strategy when the board is fixed.

To show this, assume that the second player has at least a drawing
strategy (otherwise the first player has a winning strategy). The
first player, call him or her A, follows the strategy above. While
this game is being played out, suppose that at the same time a
"shadow game" is being played in which B moves first and plays the
same moves he or she does in the real game, and A makes the replies
called for by the second-player strategy he or she is using.

At any point during the game, the real game and the shadow game will
be identical except that A will have one extra piece on the board in
the real game. When the real game ends, it will be A's turn to move
in the shadow game and there will be one vacant spot on the board, so
when A moves there, the final position of the shadow game will be the
same as that of the real game. By assumption, A has at least a draw
in the shadow game, so the same is true for the real game.

This is a surprising result (assuming, as I hope is true, that I've
got it right this time). I would have sworn that on, say, an equila-
teral triangle of size 7, a player who goes first and plays his or
her first move in the corner would be worse off than if he or she
hadn't played at all, but evidently not. Not only does this show
that for any fixed
board with an even number of spaces the first player can force a
draw, it also shows that if the first player cannot force a win,
he or she can't possibly play so badly on the first move as to be
unable to salvage a draw, no matter what the board's geometry is.
