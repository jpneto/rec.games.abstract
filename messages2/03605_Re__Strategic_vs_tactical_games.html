: Playing a tactical game like chess amounts to finding a way to do what you

This is wrong. He wants to know the difference between tactics and
strategy WITHIN the game of chess. Your explaination of a tactical
game is way off the mark, besides. Further, your implication that
chess is predominately a game of tactics is incorrect. Chess is
99% strategy, and 1% tactics -- but the tactics matter a great deal.

Tactical games involve many threats (threat = "intended combination").
In chess, combinations evolve from the following circumstances:
a) a move presents itself in which an opponent would require an
opponent at least two moves to defend the threats (double attack).
b) a position presents itself in which an opponent is compelled to
move, despite the fact that every move is a concession (zugzwang).

Tactics are short-range, forcing (forcing = "limiting the defender's
reasonable choices") plans to considerably alter the course of the
game.

Thus, square-control, material, king-security, and piece mobility are
NOT TACTICAL features -- they are STRATEGICAL considerations...

: Playing a more strategic game like go requires you to decide _what_ you
: are going to try to do when the values of the alternatives are very
: difficult to calculate.

I will not argue whether go is "more strategic" since, it's not relevant.
However, your definition of strategic is not bad.

Strategy is long-range, unforced planning, with the intention of
merely improving your position (a superior position is one which
offers superior tactical oppurtunities). Strategy is how you
improve your position when you have many options.

Thus, a player with an unsafe king (all other things being equal)
is called upon to "defend" an inferior position. The same is true
for disadvantages in material (force), reduced mobility & lack of
good square-control (space), inferior development (time), or a
damaged pawn-structure. These are STRATEGICAL elements -- the
elements of positional evaluation. Manuevering to convert these
elements into tactical oppurtunities is the essence of strategy
in chess. As stated above, such moves are very difficult to
calculate (ie Deep-Blue sometimes plays poorly when there are no
tactical oppurtunities for it to calculate, despite the fact that
they attempt to compensate by creating evaluation functions which
encourage it to simulate intelligent strategy).

Still, Purdy has demonstrated that superior tactical oppurtunities
CAN sometimes (probably very rarely) arise from a position in which
the elements of position clearly would favor the opponent. Thus,
the elements of good position are a model, or a guideline, to be
used in making positional judgements -- nothing can replace concrete,
tactical analysis of each player's oppurtunities.

: In either type of game, your opponent will
: usually try to stop you from doing what you are trying to do, and in a
: strategic game, you must be able to figure out when it is better to stop
: trying to do what you earlier decided to do and do something else that has
: since become the more profitable thing to do.

Obviously your opponent will attempt to oppose your intentions,
and obviously, it is critical to know when to oppose and when to
counter-attack. This is true regardless.

The Earth was once thought to rest upon the back of three
giant turtles...
I think it was Soltis who, in the _Inner Game of Chess_,
argued that good chess rests upon the back of the following
three abilities:

* A keen eye for tactical oppurtunities
* The ability to calculate variations
* Good positional (strategic) evaluation

I'm sure I'm not doing Soltis justice here, and I might
even be way-off on this reference, so get the book and
learn it straight from him...

Anyway, the point is, they build upon each other --
your positional evaluations are worthless if you've
overlooked your opponents threats.

In reference to Kasparov-Deep Blue, you'll not that
Kasparov attempted to give the machine nothing to fix
it's calculation upon. He merely refrains from creating
weaknesses in his position, and avoids tactics skirmishes
until the computer's position has (hopefully!) decayed
badly enough that it will not be able to adequately
respond (from an inferior position) when he switches
into attack.
