This is a very interesting subject which I've done some thinking
about, so I'm going to jump in here and say what I know, or at least
what I think I know...

It may (or may not) be the case that a pie rule of some sort could
allow Bridg-It to "twist away" from its easy winning strategy.
However that would only save the game from global predictability ---
by which I mean the ability to quickly know exactly how one person can
win. It would not save it from its local predictability of tactics:
Because the subgames of which Bridg-It is composed have a one-to-one
relationship, it is possible to "channel" your opponent's bridges in a
prescribed way. Therefore the local tactics are not going to be
entertaining, even if the global outcome can be made (by means of some
sort of pie rule, perhaps) to be in doubt.

So the real problem is the one-to-one relationship of the subgames,
resulting in a kind of symmetry that allows you to "cancel out" your
opponent's moves. This one-to-one relationship is the very structure
of Bridg-It and therefore cannot be altered without fundamentally
changing the game, in a way that it would no longer be recognizable as
Bridg-It.

If readers are interested, they may want to check out the story of
Trellis: http://home.fuse.net/swmeyers/trellis.htm. Trellis'
exactly-three-points-apart rule forces the players to immediately
abandon all predictable channeling --- the one-to-one relationship of
the subgames still exists, but the players can no longer make easy use
of it. In fact, Trellis positions get complicated very quickly, which
you'll see if you try the game out. It's fun to play (IMO) and free
of charge! :)

As my webpage describes, I only realized Trellis' curious relationship
to Bridg-It after the former was published twice. Early last fall, I
suddenly saw that Trellis was actually a "redeemed Bridg-It." Of
course it should be stressed that Bridg-It *in its original form*
cannot be redeemed (at least from local predictability), the reason
being the one-to-one relationship of its subgames.
