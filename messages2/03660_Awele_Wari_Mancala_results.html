Some time ago, I posted the following problem:

#################################################################

<< I have been working lately on an intriguing puzzle inspired by
the african game of "Awele", without much success; maybe someone
on the net will be happier with it ?

Here goes:

consider a (finite) circular list of boxes and a (finite) set of
tokens. At any time the tokens are distributed between the boxes;
a box may contain any number of tokens (including none at all).

A move consists in taking the tokens from one of the boxes and
redistributing them clockwise, one token at a time, into the boxes,
starting with the one following the emptied box - and without
omitting that box.

The question is: given to distributions A and B of the tokens,
is there ALWAYS a series of moves which can bring the game from A
to B ?

The apparent, "practical" answer is yes: I haven't been able to
come up with a counter-example yet, and I strongly doubt there is
any; on the other hand, I cannot figure any proof of that property
- except in the obvious subcase in which there are less tokens
than boxes. Also, it is very easy to bring all of the tokens
into any of the boxes.

Of course that problem is not extraordinarily general or even
useful, but it has a world of variants; and even the subproblem
with only two boxes is difficult enough, in my opinion.
When there are more (than two) boxes, to which extent can you
choose the number of tokens inside ONE box - not even considering
the entire token distribution ?
I guess a bit of number theory might help... >>

#################################################################

I have come up with a few results since then. The basic idea is to
introduce an inter-distribution distance, and to show that playing the
same series of "moves" (boxes) on two distributions (generally) reduces
that distance (one obtains two distributions that are closer two one
another). In order to transform a distribution A into a distribution B,
you don't have to worry much about A; just build a series of moves
(playing backwards) which maps some distribution on B and is long enough:
it will map (at least in some versions of the problem) EVERY distribution
onto B.

That scheme was inspired by the continuous counterpart of the game
with only two boxes. It works well on that version; in the continuous
n-boxes version with n>2, some pathological distributions (with two empty
boxes at least) may arise. The discrete versions are even trickier,
because they are less "linear" : we have to use euclidian division, and
the rests are a problem.

Nevertheless, the use of step functions allows one to solve the discrete
2-boxes version, and yields a plausible algorithm for the n-boxes version.
Only the discrete verions are developped in the sequel.

Notations
=========
n: number of boxes
C: number (or quantity) of tokens of a distribution
X, X': distributions
b(i): number of tokens of the ith box in X
b'(i): number of tokens of the ith box in X'

Distance between two distributions
==================================
"City block distance": sum of the absolute values of the differences
of the numbers of tokens in the boxes with the same rank (!):

__
\
d(X, X') = / | b(i) - b'(i) |
--

Distance : discrete version, n=2
================================
d(X, X') = 2 | b(1) - b'(1) | = 2 | b(2) - b'(2) |
(note that, in the discrete versions, the value of the distance is even).

Suppose WNLOG that we play box 1, and b(1) >= b'(1).

b(1) = 2k + r [r = 0 or 1], b'(1) = 2k' + r' [r' = 0 or 1].
d(X, X') = 2 [2(k - k') + r - r']

After the move, we obtain the distributions Xa and X'a.

d(Xa, X'a) = 2(k - k')
d(X, X') - d(Xa, X'a) = 2[(k - k') + (r - r')]

* The difference is always positive : the distance cannot increase.
* Whenever b(1) >= b'(1) + 2, the difference is STRICTLY positive :
when the distance is big enough, it decreases.


Distance : discrete version, n >= 2
===================================
Suppose again that we play box 1, and b(1) >= b'(1).

Let us split the set of the other boxes (of which the indexes belong to
{2,...,n}) in three sets S1, S2, S3:
- S1 is the set of the boxes i such that b(i) >= b'(i)
(hence b(i)a >= b'(i)a, after the move);
- S2 is the set of the boxes i such that b(i) < b'(i) and b(i)a >= b'(i)a;
- S3 is the set of the boxes i such that b(i) < b'(i) and b(i)a < b'(i)a.

Let D1, D2, D3 be the distances associated with S1, S2, S3 respectively.

A straightforward calculation shows that:

d(X, X') - d(Xa, X'a) = 2 { D2 + |S3| [(b(1) - b'(1)) : n] }

(':' denoting the euclidian division, |S3| the size of S3).

Again:
* The difference is always positive : the distance cannot increase.
* Whenever b(1) >= b'(1) + n, the difference is STRICTLY positive :
when the distance is big enough, it decreases (that point is admitedly
slightly more difficult to prove in the general case).


Alas! The distance does not necessarily decrease when it is small.
I have even found for the 2-boxes version a sequence of moves that leaves
unchanged two distinct distributions:
- sequence: 1, 1, 2, 2;
- distributions: (3, 1) and (4, 0).
In that case, the distance never decreases: it remains the same forever.


"Stairs" functions for the 2-boxes version
==========================================
A distribution is characterized by the number of tokens in the box #1,
b(1). Let us see how the two possible moves (namely 1 and 2) affect b(1).

Playing box 1: b(1)a = b(1) : 2 = f( b(1) )

Playing box 2: b(1)a = C - [ (C - b(1)) : 2 ] = g( b(1) )

Both f and g are simple "stairs" functions:

^ f(x)
|
4 |
|
3 | * *
|
2 | * *
|
1 | * *
|
0 |* *
----------------------> x
0 1 2 3 4 5 6 7

^ g(x)
|
4 | * *
|
3 | * *
|
2 |* (example: C = 4)
|
1 |
|
0 |
----------------------> x
0 1 2 3 4 5 6 7

The composition of two stairs functions a, b with stair lengths La, Lb
yields a new stairs function with stair length La * Lb, that is increasing
if a and b are both increasing or decreasing, decreasing otherwise.

Now, given a distribution D, it is fairly easy to play **backwards**,
that is to find a distribution and a move that yield D (for instance:
consider the box with the lower number of tokens; multiply it by two, and
substract it from the other box). Let us iterate that process. Since there
are finitely many distributions, we finally reach a distribution that we
have already encountered (a loop appears); we come up with a distribution
D1 and a sequence of moves s1 that maps D1 onto itself.

The stairs function f1 associated with s1 admits at least one fixed point
(corresponding to D1). Let us compose f1 with itself repetitively: the
stair associated with D1 grows wider, until it reaches at least one of
0 and C (the lower and upper bounds on b(1)). Happily, those values are
very easy to obtain: playing a box C times in a row empties it.

Hence the following algorithm: from D, play backwards, get s1 and D1; then
play s1 on (0, C) until you loop on a distribution D2, on (C, 0) until
you loop on a distribution D3. At least one of D2 and D3 is D1, a known
"ancestor" of D; therefore you come up with a known ancestor of D that is
easily reached from any distribution. QED


n-boxes version
===============
It is still feasible and easy to play back to the past. One finally
loops on a distribution D and comes up with a sequence s of moves that
maps D onto itsel. s **probably** maps at least one the concentrated
distributions (0, ..., C, 0, ...) (all of the boxes are empty, except one
that contains all the tokens) onto D, when played a number of times,
by analogy with the 2-boxes version.
It is easy to decide whether a concentrated distribution is or is not
mapped onto D by the iterations of s: a loop appears anyway; if it is
not on D, you have to try another concentrated distribution.
Those "black hole" distributions are very easy to reach, from any original
distribution.

The trouble is, the functions at work are much more complicated than the
stairs functions.
