TRIANGLE - a new impartial game

From: jonathan@jwelton.demon.co.uk ()
Date: 27 01 1998 00:00:00 GMT


Here is an amusing (I find) little impartial game which I've given the
functional name 'Triangle'.

A position in Triangle consists of three sticks of integral length
which can be arranged to form a triangle. Rather than use real sticks,
most people will find it easier to use a pencil and write down the
lengths on paper, but don't forget the triangle inequality - the sum
of two sides must be greater than the third side.

For instance suitable triangles are 3,4,5 or 7,7,7. Invalid as
positions are, for example, 3,5,8 or 6,6,19.

A move in Triangle consists of taking one of the sticks and discarding
it, breaking one of the remaining sticks into two integral parts, and
arranging these with the remaining stick to form a new triangle.

So, from 3,4,5 a valid move would be to discard the 4, and break the 5
into 2+3 to leave 2,3,3.
Or from 7,9,11 you might discard the 9, break the 11 into 4+7 and
leave 4,7,7.

The winner is the player to make the last move.

What sort of winning positions are there? Well 1,1,1 for a start - you
can discard a 1, but it's not possible to break up a 1 into two
integral parts, so 1,1,1 is a winning leave.
Perhaps less apparent is that any equilateral triangle is an immediate
win - it is never be possible to form a valid triangle after moving.

Are there any other immediate winning positions? Yes! Along with
equilateral triangles, odd sided isosceles triangles having base 1 are
immediate wins. For example, 1,3,3 or 1,19,19. No move is possible.

These are all the immediate wins, but let's see if we can find some
other non-immediate winning positions. Note that isosceles triangles
are generally good positions to leave, because they restrict our
opponent to discarding one of the equal sides and breaking the larger
side, so we'll start with a small isosceles triangle.

What if we leave 2,2,3? Our opponent's only valid move is to discard
one of the 2s, and break the 3 into 1+2 to leave 1,2,2. But then we
can discard a 2 and break a 2 to leave 1,1,1 and win. So 2,2,3 is also
a win for the previous player.

But because 2,2,3 is a win, 3,4,n is losing for any n (because we can
discard n, break 4, and play to 2,2,3). We can call 3,4 a 'losing
pair'.
Likewise, 2,5 is a losing pair (because 2,5,n allows us to discard n,
break 5 and leave 2,2,3). I would add that 1,6 is also a losing pair,
because from 1,6,n we can always play to the immediate win 1,3,3.

So 1,6 and 2,5 and 3,4 are all losing pairs. But these pairs are all
the ways in which a 7 can be broken, so if we can arrange to leave a
position in which our opponent's only move involves breaking a 7, we
must win. Such positions exist, and in fact are all the isosceles
triangles with largest side 7. So
1,7,7; 2,7,7; 3,7,7; 4,7,7; 5,7,7; 6,7,7; 4,4,7; 5,5,7; 6,6,7;
and 7,7,7 are all winning positions.

And with that flurry of winning triangles, I'll leave this brief foray
into the analysis of Triangles. Maybe somebody can analyse deeper and
answer these questions:

1) What is the smallest non-isosceles winning triangle?
2) We could call 7 'unbreakable'. 3 is also unbreakable. Are there any
other unbreakable numbers?
3) And, of course, is there a formula for identifying winning
triangles?
