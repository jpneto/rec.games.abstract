Suppose you want to toss heads or tails, but all you have is a "honest",
regular die (no coin in sight). Easy: just throw the die; 1, 2, 3 will
denote heads, 4, 5,6, tails. Since both sets are equally probable, it works
out just fine: you obtain exactly the bit of random information you want.

Now consider the reverse situation: you need a die cast, but all you have
is a coin. You have to resourt to a somewhat more complex procedure, such as
1) toss heads or tails three times;
2) interpret the three bits you come up with as the binary encoding of an
integer n in 1..8;
3) if n actually belongs to 1..6, you're done (you have your die cast);
otherwise, go back to step 1) and "play it again, Sam!".
The trouble with that method is that you can't set an upper bound on the
number of tosses it will take to get a die cast (you may even, in principle,
never succeed!).

So we have a first problem: given two "generalized" dice A and B (with nA
and nB equally likely sides respectively), can we use A to simulate B in a
certain way, thanks to a finite procedure?
The second problem deals with the efficiency of the simulation. The die
could theoretically yield log2(6) = 2.584962... tosses on average, in the
first example, and we got only 1!
How can we improve that ratio?

In fact, those two problems are not that difficult for fair dice.
- Certainty: you can simulate B with A iff there is an integer p such that
nA^p is divisible by nB (throw A p times and partition the result set in
nB parts). This, in turn, is true iff all prime divisors of nB also divide
nA.
- Efficiency: accumulate a (large) number n of casts of A, such that
nA^n > nB. In order to interpret the results, partition the output space
in subsets of nB elements (there is, in general, a remainder; the leftover
elements are to be ignored). Repeat the same procedure on the parts set,
and then on the set of sets of parts, etc. until exhaustion. You come up
with a decision tree that enables you to interpret the casts of A in terms
of casts of B (of which number depends on the branch of the tree you land
on). Note that the decision tree must be established a priori,
irrespective of the particular set of A casts you have at hand (otherwise
the B casts won't be equally distributed). [Phew!]

For instance, if the A casts have yielded 0101, you obtain two casts of B:
3 and 2.
It can be be shown that as n grows arbitrarily large, the efficiency of the
procedure (the ratio of the the average number of casts of B you obtain to
its theoretical optimal value, log nB (nA^n) = n log nA / log nB) converges
towards 1 (it becomes arbitrarily good).

Now the picture gets a lot more blurred when it comes to loaded dice, of
which sides are not equally likely. I consider only rational distributions;
a die is then represented by an unordered finite collection of stricly
positive integers, with a common greatest divisor of 1. Those coefficients
represent the likelyhoods of the sides of the die (their sum is called the
weight of the die, their number, its length; the probability of appearance
of a side is worth its coefficient divided by the weight of the die).
The product of two dice is defined as follows:
- form their "cartesian product",
- replace each pair of integers by the product of its two members.
That die is such that one cast thereof is equivalent to a cast of each of
the two original dice (the possible outputs are as numerous, and they
display the same probability distribution). [And yes, the greatest common
divisor of the coefficients of the new die IS 1.]

With those definitions in mind...

Are the following propositions decidable?
1) Given two dice loaded A and B, A **cannot** simulate B thanks to a finite
procedure.
2) Given a loaded die A and a regular die B, A **cannot** simulate B thanks
to a finite procedure (sub-case of 1)).
3) Given a loaded die A, A **cannot** simulate any regular die thanks to a
finite procedure.
[A **can** simulate B iff there is an integer n such that A^n's coefficients
can be partitioned in subsets of which weights are proportional to B's
coefficients.]

And what about the efficiency of the simulations? To which extent can the
information of the casts of a die be exploited for the simulation of
another die?

All I have so far are very partial results (a bunch of examples, in fact).
For example: {1, 2} cannot simulate (certainly) any regular die... Can you
see why?

I think there might be quite a few interesting problems in the field of
loaded dice. They could serve as illustrations for the information
theories... Also, the dice multiplication is worth a look: each dice can be
decomposed as a product of "prime" dice, but that decomposition, unlike for
integers, is in general not unique (I have discussed that question in a
couple of previous posts, which referred to "bags" instead of "dice").
