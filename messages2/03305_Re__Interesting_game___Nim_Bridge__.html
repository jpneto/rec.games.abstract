I have played this game, with a couple of slightly different rules :

In article: <4p78ad$3pv@crcnis3.unl.edu> swd@cse.unl.edu (S W Dyer)
writes:
> INSTRUCTIONS for "NIM-BRIDGE:" Assemble a deck of 20 playing cards,
> just A-K-Q-J-10 in each suit. Pick a player to go first by "leading to
> the first trick." Divide the cards evenly among the players according
> to some agreeable random process. The cards can be concealed if
> players like, or exposed, since anyone knowing one hand knows the
> other. :-)

We played with A, 10, K, Q, 7, in this order of value, but this just makes
you think a little more.

> Each turn, one player plays ("leads") a card from his/her hand, which
> the other player must "follow" with a card from his/her hand. Each
> pair of cards as such is called "one trick."
>
> To follow the lead, the second player must play a card of the same
> suit, if able to, or failing that to play a card of the same rank. A
> player unable to follow in either of these two ways forfeits the game.
>
> Each trick is "won" by the player playing the highest card of the suit
> led, the cards ranking in the order above. The winner of one trick
> leads to the next,

And if you follow rank, then the player playing the card of the higher
suit wins the trick.

> and if anyone wins the last trick, they win the game.

This is the first big difference. We played that if the game went all ten
tricks with noone being unable to play, then the game was a draw.

> OBJECT: To win this game, either win the last trick, or lead a card
> your opponent cannot follow. (Summary value only)
>
> ALTERNATE OBJECT: Since the game may not be fair to the second player,
> and since it's rather short, alternate first turns and play until
> someone wins two, three or five times, whatever you like.

This is the second big difference. After each trick, each player keeps
their own card (laying it face down infront of them on the table). When
the game is over, the players swap hands, and they play again, the other
player leading this time. So if one hand is better, or there is an
advantage to playing first, then it is reversed the second time around.

Of course, quite often the first time, the winner finds the optimal
strategy, and then the second player just plays it again exactly the same.

And as for there being an advantage to playing first, I would say it is
about as unbalenced as chess.

Finally, it is very noticable that the most common results for the two
hands, are 1 win each, 1 player wins both, or two draws. 1 win and 1 draw
is very unusual.
