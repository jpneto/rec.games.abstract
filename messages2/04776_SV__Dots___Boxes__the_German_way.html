Another nice variant that you may wish to try is Quick dots and Boxes, where
is an analogy to 2-move-games. Each player places 2 marks at a time until
one box is surrounded on 3 edges, whence the game reverts to standard dots
and boxes.

Your game is not possible to use to analyze is the same way as standard Dots
and Boxes using Combinatorial Game Theory, and it seems unlikely that it is
at all, but it is a nice challenge!!!

> German Dots and Boxes appears to be played differently from the MSRI
variant
> as described in "Games of no Chance" edited by Richard Nowakowski.
>
> Called "Ksekstchen" in German ("little cheese squares"), German kids
draw
> the edges of a rectangle on a paper sheet (with squares). There is no
fixed
> size, the "board can well number over 200 squares.
> This pre-drawn edge later becomes part of the squares conquered by the
> individual player (usually this is a two players game).
>
> "Pantherkstchen" ("little Panther squares") differs not much from this
> traditional German children's game:
>
> While in the traditional game each player draws an orthogonal line which
is
> exactly as long as the side of a square, in Pantherkstchen players draw
> straight orthogonal lines that are exactly two sides long (use the same
> pencil). Your goal is still to surround a single square of the lattice
with
> lines, which you then claim as your own (mark them with a dot or a cross).
> If you surround a square or several single squares at once, you can
> immediatly draw another straight line (always two sides long!) and
continue
> as long as you conquer squares each time. Each turn ends with a line which
> doesn't conquer squares (except the very last move in the game). You must
> move. The player who conquers most squares wins. Capturing (i.e. claiming
> squares) is not compulsory. At the end of the game you also have neutral
> squares: squares which are not and cannot completely surrounded. The game
is
> only meant to be played by extremely intelligent kids (sort of a joke :-))
,
> so try this game on a very large board. I consider 25x25=625 squares as
the
> standard size (this is not one of these ugly capitalist games for hunted
> people who never have enough time!). The conquered squares are considered
to
> be "land" owned by a player, while the squares are interpreted as "water"
> (lakes, ocean) which doesn't belong to any player.
>
> This is a nice territory game and would surely be a cute challenge for
game
> theorists...
