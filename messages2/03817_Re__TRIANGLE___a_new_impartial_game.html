I'm sure these results are correct, since they agree with what I
determined by hand, although your program went much further than I
did. I suspect my algorithm is more efficient though. In fact, I
believe it to require not much more than constant time for each
triangle.

I can send you details on request, but essentially, the method is to
construct a 2D table of pairs m,n labeling them 'losing', if one of
the pair can be broken to produce a winning triangle, or 'safe'
otherwise. This can be done very rapidly if one has a list of winning
triangles of perimeter m+n (m,n is losing if the smaller of m and n
appears as a side in the list).
The table of pairs is built up a diagonal (where m+n=constant) at a
time. After each diagonal, we can read off winning triangles for each
safe pair m,n by looking for numbers p which are safe with both m and
n. The hardest part is checking for the triangle inequality!

Of course, the disadvantage of working by hand is that it is prone to
error as I demonstrated, leaving the values I calculated above about
perimeter 25 suspect.

The advantage of working by hand is that other trends become apparent.
For example: it appears that 3,m,n is a win precisely when 2,m,n is.
Likewise with 4,p,q and 6,p,q.
I think there should be a simple proof of this, but for the life of me
I can't see it. It is most mysterious.

>Out of the smallest 100 triangles, 42 are winning. Out of the first
>1000, 218 are winning and 369 out of the first 2000 are. So the
>density of winning positions is (not surprisingly) declining.

>If we simplify the game by dropping the requirement of keeping the
>triangle inequality, then it appears that if (a,b,c) is a winning
>position then so is (n*a,n*b,n*c) for any integer n>0.

The simplified, unconstrained, game can be expressed as a game using
heaps of counters:
A position consists of 3 (non-zero) heaps of counters, and a move is
to discard one heap and break one of the other heaps into two smaller
heaps.

I believe the P-positions (Previous player wins) to be those in which
the highest power of two dividing the number of counters in a heap is
the same for each heap.

I'm not sure how the rule extends to 4 or more heaps.
