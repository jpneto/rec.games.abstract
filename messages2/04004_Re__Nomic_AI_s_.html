The problem I see with most traditional AI techniques applied to nomic is
that nomic is not so much a game as a toy. That is, nomic lacks a single
goal that all players strive for; rather, it is an interesting system that
the participants play with to see what happens and to see what they can
make it do. Nomic is not so much something you play as something you play
with.

As such, no assumptions can be made about any of the players' goals, or
even if they have goals, for that matter. For the most parts, the players
try to make the "game" have behavior that they consider interesting or
amusing, or to reach some goal they have set for themselves.

In this environment, the players' first step is not to find the best
heuristics to meet their goal, but to analyze the rules, and pick out a
goal that would provide them with the most enjoyment in attempting to
accomplish. Since the computer experiences equal utility, no matter what
the rules change to be, it has no basis to make this decision on, and so
even if the computer could understand the rules -- this is the trivial
part, mind you, a nomic was once implemented in Perl -- it still has no
reason to play, and no basis to decide whether it is playing well or
poorly.
