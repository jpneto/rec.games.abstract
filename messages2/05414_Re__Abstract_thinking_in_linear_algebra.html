I think a certain amount of that is inevitable in an
undergraduate course. It won't really sink in until you
are using it in a non-course context (which might include
simply a different course from the one where you learned it).
In full-time undergraduate study, there simply isn't much time
to be looking at any applications except your next course.

Perhaps you might enjoy reading on your own over the summer,
either a more advanced abstract text (such as Real Analysis,
perhaps the text by Rudin) or even some of the research
literature.

> I have a few things I wish I could see before I'm too old to enjoy it:
>
> 1. I wish I could see logic and math taught together ... throw in a
> little set theory too... at the high school level, and carried through
> at each step of the way.

This was tried while I was going through school. I was
drawing Venn diagrams in first grade. I think the experiment
by and large was regarded as a failure. For one thing, the
abstract stuff was emphasized and basic arithmetic was
dropped. That led to kids who can't take 10% of a bill in their
heads. And nobody can make change anymore.

> 2. Give people the option to learn physics, analysis and calculus
> together over a period of time much much longer than presently. Call
> it a combined masters program in applied math or something... give it
> about 7 years. Or maybe we need to admit that a 4 year program isn't
> very workable in math? Let's face it.. .it's a much harder subject
> than almost any other.

It's as workable as any other technical subject. Yes, somewhere
around 1 or 2 years out of school, it will all sink in. But that
doesn't mean your undergraduate training was wasted.

> 3. Why should professors have to teach at breakneck speed and
> practically not have time to answer questions in class? Shouldn't a
> math class take at least 2-3 hours to allow for discussion,
> reflection, thought, etc?

The realities of scheduling. Graduate courses have the same
amount of contact time. But generally you have much more
out of class contact, both with other students and with faculty,
for mathematical discussions.

> 4. Professors who yell or belittle students in class or out of it
> should simply not be allowed to teach.

Where do you attend school? This sounds like a deliberately
competitive program.

> I think I'm experiencing many of the downsides to the current system.

> reality and move on into the applied math world... ( numerical

If you can stick with it AND learn numerical methods, algorithm
design, or some other applied area such as statistics or
