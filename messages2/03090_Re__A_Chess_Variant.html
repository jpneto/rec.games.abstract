In article <43h6oe$m50@jaxnet.jaxnet.com> beckwith@jax.jaxnet.com (Matthew) writes:
>Have any of you ever played the kind of chess where each player only sees
>her own pieces? Usually you use three boards and have a moderator.
>The two players sit facing away from each other, each with her own board
>with only her own pieces. A third board is placed between, and contains
>all the pieces.
>One player moves. The moderator makes that move on the central board.
>If a capture takes place, then the moderator goes to the opponent's board
>and removes that piece.
>I think this would be a delightful game to play, but haven't found anyone
>to play it with me yet.
>It would be ideal for implementation on one of the internet chess
>servers. I suggested it, but no one has done it yet.

Use to play this all the time at work during lunch.

1) an experienced ref doesn't need the third board. Our usual games
were played with just two boards with a barrier between and the ref
sitting to the side so he could see both boards.

2) our rules had a VERY small set of things the ref was allowed to say
during the course of a game. A player would attempt a move and ref
would could responed:

NO illegal move, usually because a long distance moving piece
tried to jump an opposing piece. player typically try probably
illegal moves to probe the board for the opponents pieces.

HELL NO the move would be illegal even if the player could see all the
pieces and should have known that,

BLACK/WHITE telling the other player his opponent has completed a `
legal move and he should now move

PIECE/PAWN GONE legal move resulted in a capture, the player was told
when he captured but not what piece.

CHECK ON RANK/FILE/DIAGONAL legal move resulted in check

Also to speed up the game the ref would inform a player, before he moved,
as to possible pawn captures.

It is also usefull to give both players a complete set of both colors of
pieces. The only "note taking" we allowed was to allow a player to arrange
his copy of his opponent's pieces on his own board to keep track of what
he believes is the possition of his opponent's pieces.
