This quote was made by German player Richard Teichmann (1868-1925). I am
not sure in which book or environment he made this comment.I might
suggest you post the question at Hanlon Russell's website
(http://www.chesscafe.com/) in the bulletin board and someone there or
Hanlon Russell himself might know from what text the quote
originated.Well as to whether I agree with the quote...I disagree. A
player obviously must have a through understanding of the types of
tactics which exist in chess,and how to apply them,however to say that
all games are decided by tactics (which is essentially what this quote
is saying to my mind) is not correct.Chess consists of strategy and
tactics and other factors as well. If a player were to only focus on
looking for tactics in a position,then he or she could be in a
strategically lost position within a very short time.To play chess at a
high level requires skill in many different aspects of the game,not just
tactics.I have played through countless games of chess,which have been
annotated by some of the greatest chess players ever to play the
game,and many times I have seen the annotator use the term "white (or
black) has a "strategically lost position." In other words..the strategy
used by one player,was superior to the strategy used by the other.Long
range planning (which is what strategy basically is) is essential to
succeed. This has been the focus of a lot of debate in relation to chess
computers and chess playing computer programs.Chess programmers have
been able to create programs which excel at tactics,however these
programs are not as proficient at long range strategy.It is this lack
of ability to see and plan many moves in advance, which is the achilles
heal of chess playing programs.However,as computer programs evolved,it
was possible to program them to learn from their mistakes.So that the
strategic component to their play has been improved in recent years.

To quote GM Yermolinsky from his book, "The Road to Chess Improvement",
a strategic plan can be conducted by tactical means,independent of the
postional principles it was based on.It just doesn't happen very
ofen,that is why we often confuse "strategic" with tactical." Further on
in this book,Yermolinsky summarizes what we must do in our minds in the
middle game.To paraphrase Yermolinsky, we first must have the ability to
extend opening theory and apply it to a position,we must have knowledge
of the positional elements of chess,and we must have knowledge of
endgame positions.And this is in addition to our ability to calculate
variations (until we can evaluate and come to some conclusion in our
mind concerning the position).Yermolinsky then says that another key
factor in playing chess successfully is to possess intuition (to feel
that some position are right or wrong) because we look at conflicting
positional elements and decide which one will prevail. Also there is a
great deal of psychology which occurs during a game and this is what
Yermolinsky considers to be the third "weapon" "we have at our disposal"
in evaluating a middle game position...such things as the ability to get
in the mind of our opponent and look at the position from his side,and
also among other things,to deal with the fact that we have a certain
history against this player (possibly if we play in a club and play this
person frequently during competition). In summary,to my way of
thinking,chess and success in chess,requires a great deal more than
simply tactical play.It is a players ability to coordinate his or her
knowledge of tactics and strategy,as well as having knowlege of opening
play and endgame play that is important.And a player must possess the
necessary psychological and personal qualities to deal with
psychological stress and others stresses which occur during a game and a
tournament.
