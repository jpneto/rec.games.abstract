This argument is called "strategy-stealing." Yes, it serves to prove
that the first player should win in 'standard' Hex. However, Hex has
been implemented on http://www.playsite.com with an additional rule.
Specifically, after the very first token is played by Black, White has
the option of swapping that token. the swapped token changes color and
moves to its mirror-image position as reflected on the long diagonal.
For example, if A1 is an acute corner, then C2 swaps to B3, I9 stays
on I9, etc. This is effectively the same as swapping sides. Another
name for this rule is "one-move equalization." It can be applied to a
wide variety of two-player games. With this rule in place, the second
player has a win; all that is necessary is to correctly decide whether
to swap or not after the first move is made. In practice, however,
there is some debate over which moves should be swapped and which
shouldn't. It is pretty well agreed, and may have been proven, that
the first move in the acute corner should not be swapped. (Note that
this is not an exception to the above statement "an extra piece cannot
hurt you." It is better to be the first player in a game that starts
1.A1 2.F6 than to be the second player against 1.F6 in a game where
swap is not allowed.) Other first moves are unclear; Hex fuseki
patterns haven't been worked out very far yet, as far as I know (which
isn't much.)

I would also like to take this opportunity to invite Go players to
check out the game of Twixt. It's a connection game, similar to Hex,
but on a square grid. Each player has two types of pieces: pegs and
links. Links connect two pegs of yours that are at opposite corners of
a 6-holed rectangle, like a knight's move in chess. This results in a
lot more complexity and variety than Hex provides. For example, draws
are possible, albeit rare. Also, handicapping is feasible by changing
one dimension of the board. This doesn't work in Hex; there is an
elegant and simple winning strategy if you have fewer rows to span
than your opponent in Hex, even if you move second. Anyway, Twixt is
not available (yet) on Playsite. but it is available on the
play-by-email server at http://www.gamerz.net/~pbmserv which supports
lots of other email games, including Go and Hex. The complete and
correct rules of Twixt are listed there, as well as a series of 40
puzzles.
