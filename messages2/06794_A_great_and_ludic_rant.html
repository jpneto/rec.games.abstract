Greetings all,

For the purpose of this rant, I immediately dismiss what has come to call
itself ludology. As far as I know, this young science is first and foremost
concerned with the narrative structures of computer games. This is indeed a
respectable field of study, and undeniably part of the science of games.
However, I imagine a broader (or should I say muddier) branch of ludic
knowledge. (Pardon the phoney language ;)

Games are divided in their relation to the rest of the world. On the one
hand, every game and indeed the basic principle of games have wordly
origins. On the other hand, games are autonomous and have nothing to do with
the world.

There are inumerable ways in which games are entagled with the subtle and
ambiguous systems of the physical world (and the psychology of players).

Which came first: the die, or the notion of chance?

I like to imagine that at some point when man was very young, and used his
body to measure what was going on, a person seeing a falling leaf, for
instance, asked himself on which side the leaf would land. It seems to me
that mechanical randomness is a principle more readily grasped than the
fuzzy ideas of fate, chance etc. We might often see a die or a deck of cards
as a mechanical model of coincidence, but in the end we all know that the
egg came before the hen :) However it may be, dice and similar "tools" of
chance have certainly shaped our ideas of the principles they embody. In an
imagined world without dice, chance would mean something else than what it
means to us.

On a similar note, historians speculate to whether some games may have
originated from divination boards. This does not simply imply that the
origin of (even deterministic) games is connected to the origin of chance;
if some games were played on boards which were actually
astrological/geomantical representations, it means that the games must in
the beginning have had some (vague?) connection to the starred heavens and
the earth. Who can tell - if it was really so, we can probably never
understand the extremely subtle implications it had the first time a diviner
sat down to play a game with his tools.

At some point, different games took on different allegorical meanings.
Ancient Chinese thinkers compared chess to war in a hierarchical society, go
to a battle between two minds, backgammon to the struggles of men in a world
controlled by inhuman principles (the principle of chance, or of the gods).
Confucius were probably closer to the truth when he said that playing liubo
or yi is (slightly) better than doing nothing. Comparing games to war is now
the rule rather than the exception. The names of the chess pieces imply yet
another system of allegory. The knight as the cavalry that moves swiftly and
breaks through the enemy's lines, etc. Obviously, blatant allegorical
readings of games say very little about what is really going on, a a
gameplay level. I think it was Roman Jacobson who used chess to exemplify
how the sign is in itself random. The bishop has nothing to do with "real"
bishops (and in different languages is called different things: the runner,
the madman, etc). Except for maybe some general and vague "feeling". But if
we called the bishop "the H0pp'l" and represented it as an empty beer bottle
on the chess board, the game would be exactly the same.

However, chess pieces do undeniably have some sort of "personalities". Would
it be wrong to say that the knight is a nimble piece? This may still be
based on the players' feel for the game, but it's definately more signifying
of what the knight "is all about" than the equine piece of wood. The bishops
have the fascinating property that they can only move on half the board.
Saying this amounts to a great deal, and a person who never played chess but
was only explained the rules might not even consider the fact that it is so.
In go, you have principles like eyes and ko which are so basic applications
of the rules that some people almost forget that they are not actually
rules, but concequences - aspects of the nature of go. (Then you have ko
rules and such, which is another matter entirely - the principle of ko, the
basic position, must have astonished the first pair of players who
encountered it.)

This nature has nothing to do with how the games are entangled with the
social and elemental world. On the contrary: it shows itself to those who
play at the game, as a set of absolute laws which rise from an unambiguous
system of rules.

So, the ambiguous way in which games relate to the world has to do with the
fact that games are not at all ambiguous; they are deeply autonomous.

A play is made at once in the "world" (where for instance psychological
factors between the players interact) and in a closed and purified, "ludic"
space. The sequence of moves can be written down and circulated as a system
of signs, and the moves will lose nothing of their ludic significance. In
relation to the game, a move can be analysed 1000 years after it was made
(and still, you have examples like Shusaku's "ear reddening move" (in go),
which is more famous for its psychosomatical impact than because those who
remember it actually understand its deeper, autonomous significance :)

Do games have an inner, unambiguous nature? If so, will people ever be able
to understand this nature? Even though some ludic principles may be
expressed unambiguously, there must certainly be aspects which can only be
grasped by signifying terms, but still convey some basic truth. Saying that
the chess-knight is nimble amounts to nothing if not allegory. Still, one
cannot really deny it as such. What is the deep and secret nature of ko?
What does it even mean that so many deterministic games are prone to get
stuck in loops? (Maybe this can say something for the origin of the die -
games of chance will never repeat themselves indefinately.)

Game theorists solve games. But they are probably no closer to a true
"vision" of the game than the computers crunching their way through the
variations. Solving a game may be compared to solving a riddle: The riddle
*is* really dissolved by the solution (like algebra always returns zero when
the solution is formulated; saying x=13 amounts to saying 13=13), and the
immediate interest in the conundrum fades. But there is something more to
riddles, a good riddle is always an enigmatic formulation of some concept,
and even when we solve the riddle, does that mean we understand the nature
of enigma?

Does any of this make sense? Did anyone read the entire post? :-P
