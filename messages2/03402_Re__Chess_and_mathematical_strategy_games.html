(Jonathan Hayward) wrote:

>I was reading _Godel,_Escher,_Bach:_the_Eternal_Golden_Braid_ today, and musing
>about chess and other strategy games, and formal systems...
>
>It could be said that chess is a formal system. It is a formal system which
>has as its axioms the initial board setup and a boolean flag indicating whose
>turn it is, and inference rules which tell what are legal moves to make,
>moving the piece and toggling the flag. A game is a theorem, in which each
>move is a step. The objective of each player, of course, is to make an
>inference at each level so that the result proven will be the opponent in a
>checkmate.
>
>(Other strategy games are essentially the same in this regard.)
>
>There are a million different directions which could be taken from this
>observation... The one I'd like to take is...
>
>What about a formal system (still a game) in which the axioms and inference
>rules taken together make it possible to prove an inconsistency?
>
>(I'm not entirely sure that a system can prove an inconsistency without having
>some interpretation... perhaps a better question to ask is, "What about a
>formal system in which the axioms and inference rules make it possible to prove
>anything?" That might be a condition for winning, or it might allow the player
>to modify the axioms, rules, or state of theorem according to some
>specifications, or...)
>
>This sounds vaguely like NOMIC...
>
>Does anyone else have musings along this or related lines?

One might be able to stretch Godel's Theorem and say that may be possible
that their are legal chess positions that cannot be reached by following
the rules.
