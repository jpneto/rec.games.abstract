Devin P. Mccullen <dqm5431@is4.nyu.edu> wrote:

Probably the same qualities I'd associate with a good film, book, or
music critic (because I think that's what were talking about here -
high-quality criticism). This would include: a knowledge of the
history of the field, both in terms of specific games, companies, and
how board gaming has changed; a willingness to try something new, and a
desire to find new and interesting games; someone who recognizes his
own biases and tries to overcome them and make readers aware of them
(This is why Michael Medved annoys me); someone who doesn't look at the
popularity, or lack thereof, of a game in judging it (this is why
virtually all rock music critics annoy me no end); and, most
importantly, someone who can explain to a large audience the qualities
of the game and why she liked or disliked it, which is porbably the
most difficult skill to master.

Incidentally, in one issue of The Duelist, Richard Garfield was
discussing the problems of reviewing games, and one of the points that
he made was that, unlike other fields of criticism, game reviewers
don't have a common set of terms or background references that they can
draw upon to explain games, and that the only way this will change is
over time. (To clarify this, in game reviews tehre generally aren't
terms that reviewers can use, as simple as "The acting was extremely
STIFF", or "Their songs are unbelievably TRITE". Outside of the broad
fields, (RPG, CCG, war-gaming, etc.) these terms generally don't exist
in gaming). As game reviewers build up a critical literature, such
terms will come into use, which will make criticism that much more
simple and clear. I've probably mangled Garfield's argument here, so
direct all criticism to me.

You know, the more I hear about Richard Garfield, the more I like him.
When "Magic" first came out, I thought he was a brilliant marketing
strategist with a fair game on his hands. Then, when "Magic" became such
a bizarre phenomenon, I began to dislike him, seeing the game as an
aberration that I hoped would vanish. Now that I'm hearing more about his
philosophies of gaming, I realize that he really knows where it's at. At
least, philosophically.

Part of the difficulty with the language of games lies in the fact that
the field of gaming is still in its infancy, due to the fact that games
are still not considered a serious subject of study. As you point out,
gamers don't even have a critical language with which to _critique_ games,
much less to design them. We once had a discussion on rec.games.design
arguing what the term "complexity" meant--there was no consensus, although
I believe I made a convincing argument for my definition. Until the study
of games improves I don't believe the quality of games will advance at
anything more than a crawl.
