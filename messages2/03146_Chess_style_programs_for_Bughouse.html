Chess-style programs for Bughouse

From: colley@qucis.queensu.ca (Paul Colley)
Date: 13 12 1995 00:00:00 GMT

Bughouse chess is not strictly an abstract game; there is "limited
information", namely, how fast is the game going to progress on each
board? Or more accurately, how long will one of the opposing players
stall with his or her clock running to frustrate the plan on the other
board?

"Limited Information" doesn't seem to be quite the right phrase: Every
player knows exactly how much time there is, and all the state
information, and there's no random factor. The problem is that it's
not clear who is to move: Either board could have the next move made.
If it involves players on opposing teams, then in the game-theoretic
sense it's not clear which team will move first.

You can have race conditions, where both sides would like to move
first, and the faster player wins, and also "Chicken" conditions, when
each team would like the other to go first, and which player is willing
to sit there with their clock running the longest?

The traditional tools of computer chess work best only with two-player,
zero-sum, complete information games.

Bughouse is not a traditional abstract game. However, it can certainly
be viewed as a two-player game, since both members of the team have
identical information, they can be viewed as a single player (unlike,
e.g., Bridge). And it's zero sum. And it's very close to complete
information.

So it's *almost* an abstract game. Why not modify it to make it
abstract? I.e., remove the race conditions.

If you modify bughouse to make it fit the pattern of an abstract game,
then it seems likely that a strong bughouse player could be made for
the game.

However, doing so would probably remove much of the fun of bughouse
chess. <sigh> Anyways, here is an attempt:

(1) Normal set-up, but only two players.
(2) Players alternate which board they move on.

I.e., player 1 is white on board A, and black on board B. Player 2 has
the opposite colours.

Player 1 moves on board A.
Player 2 moves on board B.
Player 1 moves on board B.
Player 2 moves on board A.
.
.
.
etc.

Captures may be dropped as usual.

I'm not suggesting this is the most fun way to play bughouse, just that
it allows the typical chess machinery to be applied to the problem.

If you want a four-player version, just have two entities for each
"player", each of which makes the moves on a particular board. From a
game-theoretic sense (and from a computer-programming sense) you still
decide your move as if you were playing both sides.

If you want to write a program for traditional bughouse, with
race-conditions, I think you're going to have to use somewhat
different techniques than in traditional chess.
