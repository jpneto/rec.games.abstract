>So there was one player who won 62 games out of 99, and likewise one
>player who won only 39 games and 99, and various numbers of players
>who won between 41 and 61 games, following a standard bell-curve.
>
>What intrigued me is that someone looking at the above table would be
>perfectly well forgiven for thinking "well, clearly the player who won
>62 games out of 99 is a far better player than the one who won only 39
>games out of 99", and yet the program was specifically written to
>ensure that any given 'player' had a 1/2 chance of winning any game.
>
>So how much does this actually take away from player (or team)
>rankings in real life? Put another way, how accurate is any ladder or
>rankings table at judging someone's skill level, and indeed chances of
>winning? Surely this should affect the gambling odds - if you saw the
>above table, would you bet more money on the player who won 62 games,
>than the one who only won 39?
>
>Obviously the situation becomes more complicated for both games that
>involve more than 2 players at a time, and for rankings systems that
>take into account the ranking of the players you beat in order to
>assign a new ranking. But I'm not sure any system could possibly
>completely counteract the sheer fact that statistically, some players
