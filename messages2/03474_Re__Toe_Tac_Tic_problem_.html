Tord responds:

> It is a forced win for the first player.

This may be so: but I remember playing 4x4x4 on a plexiglass board set-up,
using two-colored golf-tees as markers, when I was in college (Oxford),
and the "forced win" strategy wasn't particularly apparent.

I suspect we played a quick game back then, rather than a considered one,
and have the impression that the game was effectively a test of
visualization skills rather than eg knowledge, since being able to scan
each horizontal and vertical game plane and the diagonals quickly without
missing any of them or duplicating one's efforts -- or carrying sufficient
"maps" of the state of play of each in memory -- was quite a feat for most
people.

I recognize that there's an interest in the math side of predictable
outcomes, and that some games won't particularly interest some
mathematicians if the outcome can be too easily predicted.

I also don't think there would be much joy in *playing* a game which was
strongly skewed in favor of one player, let alone one in which a forced
win is *easily seen* -- so the math should be of interest to players as
well as those for whom it's interesting in its own right -- but I'd still
be interested to hear comments on whether other players *enjoy* playing
4x4x4...

I'd also be interested in any comments on the possibility of
discriminating between games where a "forced win" solution is so obvious
that there's no point in play, and games where a "forced win" may exist
from a mathematical standpoint, but the mental-computational power
required to invent [or memorize or follow] it renders it effectively
non-existent *for recreational game purposes*.

Since the issue of "mental-computational power required" would in fact
need to take many different cognitive styles into account (eg the 3D
visualizing power in 4x4x4), this might turn out to be quite a complex
affair...
