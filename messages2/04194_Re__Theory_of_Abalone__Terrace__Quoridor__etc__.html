It's quite common for an endgame to have its empty squares concentrated
near the four corners. Unfortunately it's rare for the four regions to
be entirely independent. However, the strategy of playing to get the
last move in each region turns out to be an amazingly good rule of
thumb in Othello endgames. It goes by the name of "parity" since it
often suggests that one should play in regions with an odd number of
squares and avoid regions with an even number of squares. Parity is a
much more reliable guide than, say, the strategy of trying to seize as
many corner squares as possible.

It is also notable that zugzwang is a big part of Othello; there are
many times, especially in the opening and middle game, when it is a
disadvantage to have the move. This is because the single most
important strategic concept in Othello (until the endgame) is
"mobility"---having a large number of moves available. Typically,
making a move reduces your mobility because it reduces the number of
your opponent's disks and hence the number of moves you can make.
This seems to suggest that Othello is "colder" than something like go.

All this makes me optimistic that combinatorial game theory will have
something useful to say.
