Certainly one can do such a thing, with no real "infinite"
difficulties. Just parallel the standard construction of games:

On day -1 there are no games.

On day n, construct games as pairs (L | R) with L and R both
(finite) sets of probability distributions on the games from day n-1.

As you point out, games like Backgammon fit naturally in to this
set up. Unfortunately, the theory is unlikely to be useful. The
power of the theory for normal games comes from the fact that games
often split into sums of smaller pieces, and those pieces are often
simple enough to be completely understandable. At the heart of the
matter is that many games are zero. Unfortunately it's not hard to
see that the only games which are zero here are those where the mover
loses with probability 1 i.e. there's no randomness left. This
"failure" is not really a random vs. deterministic thing, you get
the same problem for most variants (misere play, "ko", etc). In
some ways it's not even really objective - many normal play games
fail to break down into pieces is any useful way (chess, go, phutball,
sprouts, hex, etc). These games cannot be usefully attacked this way.
(Well, go does decompose late in the endgame, when all ko's are gone
-exactly the situation that has been analysed by Berlekamp and Wolfe.)
It hust happens that some games people find interesting do decompose,
and do so into pieces whose values are relatively simple.
